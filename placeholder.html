Materials

Items were generated based on the three themes uncovered by Callari and Puppione (2025) in their qualitative study on the way employees perceive meaningful work in tasks co-generated by Microsoft 365 Copilot specifically. They identified three ideal types, including an Efficiency-Seeking Type (perceives the AI tool as a straightforward task-assistance tool), a Pragmatic Integrator Type (perceives AI tool as a smarter assistant), and a Collaborative Optimiser Type (perceives AI tool an expert-like teammate). Three different methods of item generation were used (and compared) here.

Expert-Generated AI Adopter Type Items.

Three of the researchers independently generated 6-8 items per dimension. Combined lists of items were discussed by dimension, and the most unique and clearly-written items were retained for the survey. See Appendix A.

ChatGPT-Generated AI Adopter Type Items.

The following prompt was input into ChatGPT version: “I need your help with generating a tentative list of survey items. I am reading the attached paper for the third time. I am interested in the three AI user ideal types and the survey items that could be generated to measure an employee’s propensity toward one type or another.” Eight items were generated per dimension, along with rationale for those item choices. See Appendix B for the full ChatGPT prompt and output, and Appendix C for a full list of items.

AI-GENIE-Generated AI Adopter Type Items.

AI-GENIE is a tool for automated item development and validation using large language models and network psychometrics [@russellgenerative]. The following initial prompt was input, “You are an expert scale developer and methodologist who writes high quality and succinct (although with a use a 7th grade reading level) items based on the overarching definition of AI Adopter Type as articulated in Callari & Puppione (2025): “Three Ideal Types were identified: Ideal Type [1] – the Efficiency-Seeking Type – perceives AI as a straightforward task-assistance tool, Ideal Type (2) – the Pragmatic Integrator Type – views AI as a smarter assistant, and Ideal Type (3) – the Collaborative Optimiser Type – considers AI an expert-like teammate.” Definitions of all three of the above dimensions, as well as example descriptors are provided as part of the prompt. Thirty items were initially generated. Combined lists of items were discussed by the researchers by dimension, and the most unique and clearly-written items were retained for the survey. See Appendix D for the R Script and Appendix E for the items.

Demographics.

Students were asked to report their major, age, gender, work status, and job title.

Procedure

After consent, students asked to think of the context where they use AI the most frequently, either work or school for example, as a student or as an employee, and to keep that role in mind for the entire survey. Respondents were shown items grouped by method, followed by the demographics. They responded to the AI-Adopter items on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree). Following the survey, respondents were debriefed and offered course credit for their time.

124 undergraduate students responded (mean age = 21.58, sd = 5.61). The students were given the choice of 50 different majors to choose from, with the greatest representation being Psychology at 38.71% (see Table 2). When asked, “Which context did you have in mind as you responded to the statements in this survey?”, 79.03% indicated “school”, 12.9% indicated “work”, and 7.26% stated “other”.

Each figure shows the results of an exploratory factor analysis as a network: maximum likelihood estimation with an oblimin rotation for the 2- and 3-factor solutions (the 1-factor solution has nothing to rotate). The large grey nodes are the extracted factors. The smaller coloured nodes are the survey items, and their colour shows which subscale the item was written for: Efficiency Seeking, Pragmatic Integrator or Collaborative Optimiser. A line between an item and a factor means the item loads on that factor at |.30| or higher. Thicker, darker lines mean stronger loadings, and red lines mean negative loadings. Items with no strong loading are shown unconnected. If the items worked as intended, each factor would be surrounded by a cluster of items of a single colour, and each item would connect to only one factor. Factors that collect items of mixed colours, or items that connect to several factors (cross-loadings), suggest the intended three-type structure is not holding up. Comparing the 3-, 2- and 1-factor versions shows whether a simpler structure describes the item pool better.

On the other hand, the EGAs do not specify factors in advance. Instead they estimates a network directly among the items. Each line is a regularized partial correlation (EBICglasso), meaning the relationship between two items after controlling for all the other items. Thicker lines are stronger associations, and the line colour shows whether the association is positive or negative. Weak relationships are shrunk to zero and dropped. The Walktrap algorithm then detects communities: groups of items that are more tightly connected to each other than to the rest of the network. Unlike in the EFA plots, the node colours here mark these empirically detected communities, not the intended subscales. The number of communities is the EGA estimate of the dimensionality of each item set.

“What is your rationale for generating the items you did? And why this prompt and response format?”

My rationale was to convert the paper’s qualitative ideal types into a tentative quantitative item pool while staying close to the authors’ conceptual structure. I treated the items as indicators of propensity toward an AI-use orientation, not as a validated scale or as a forced classification. Core rationale The paper identifies three ideal types of AI users: the Efficiency-Seeking Type, who views Copilot as a task-assistance tool; the Pragmatic Integrator Type, who views it as a smarter assistant; and the Collaborative Optimiser Type, who views it as an expert-like teammate. My items were designed to operationalize those three orientations in survey form. I built the items around four dimensions the authors use to distinguish the types: AI-enabled work practices and adaptation, human agency/autonomy/decision-making, teamwork in human-AI environments, and organizational support. The reason the items cover more than simple “AI usefulness” is that the paper’s typology is not only about whether employees like AI. It is about how employees position AI in relation to their own expertise, autonomy, work practices, decision-making, and relationships with others. Why those specific item themes? For the Efficiency-Seeking Type, I emphasized routine task completion, time savings, limited prompting, caution about reliance, and preservation of human judgment. That follows the paper’s description of this type as seeing Copilot as “just a tool” that supports basic task execution, efficiency, and bounded use. For the Pragmatic Integrator Type, I emphasized AI as a starting point, assistant, brainstorming partner, productivity enhancer, and support for decision-making while retaining human responsibility. This reflects the paper’s middle category: employees who do not merely automate routine work but use AI to extend their capability, organize thinking, and refine outputs. For the Collaborative Optimiser Type, I emphasized AI as a partner, challenger, sparring partner, expert-like teammate, and tool for deeper analysis. This follows the paper’s description of this type as involving more iterative, cognitively demanding, and collaborative interaction with AI. Why the prompt: “When I use AI tools at work, I generally…” I used that prompt because it does three useful things. First, it keeps the items behaviorally and contextually anchored. The construct is not general AI attitude; it is workplace AI-use orientation. Second, it avoids overcommitting to Microsoft Copilot specifically. The paper is about M365 Copilot, but your future survey may involve AI tools more broadly. “AI tools at work” keeps the measure adaptable while preserving the workplace context. Third, it frames the items as propensity statements rather than ability, frequency, or moral judgment. That matters because an employee could be capable of using AI collaboratively but still prefer to use it only for routine efficiency. Why a Likert agreement format? A Likert-type response format is appropriate at this early stage because you are trying to estimate degree of endorsement for each orientation. I would use something like: 1 = Strongly disagree 2 = Disagree 3 = Somewhat disagree 4 = Neither agree nor disagree 5 = Somewhat agree 6 = Agree 7 = Strongly agree I would avoid a forced-choice format at this stage because the paper’s ideal types are analytical constructions, not necessarily mutually exclusive categories. The authors explicitly use ideal-type analysis to preserve distinctive user perspectives and capture varied patterns of AI adoption. In practice, an employee may score high on both Pragmatic Integrator and Collaborative Optimiser tendencies. Why 8 items per type? The 8-item-per-type structure was intentional for item development. At the early item-pool stage, I would rather overgenerate slightly than prematurely narrow the construct. The goal is to give you enough item coverage to later examine: • factor structure, • item-total correlations, • cross-loadings, • reliability, • discriminant validity among the three types, • and whether a shorter 3–5 item version per type can be retained. So the first version is not meant to be the final scale. It is a developmental item pool. Bottom line The items were generated by translating the paper’s qualitative typology into survey indicators of three AI-use orientations: tool-based efficiency, assistant-based integration, and teammate-based collaboration. Growth Need Strength (GNS)

“As noted earlier, there is now substantial evidence that differences among people moderate how they react to their work, and individual need strength appears to be a useful way to conceptualize and measure such differences. The basic prediction is that people who have high need for personal growth and development will respond more positively to a job high in motivating potential than people with low growth need strength.” [@hackman1976motivation, p. 258]

Options for measurement:

Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) Growth Need Strength Scales, @hackman1975development

  1. “Job Choice” GNS Scale
  • Respondents choose between pairs of hypothetical jobs.
  • Measures preference for challenging, growth-oriented work versus comfort, security, or other job features.
  • I have the original article, but not the items themselves for the job choice measure– these two scales seem to first be described here: <https://werkwelzijnenmotivatie.wordpress. com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/hackman-oldham-1975.pdf>
  1. “Would-Like” GNS Scale
  • Respondents indicate how much challenge, responsibility, learning, autonomy, and growth they would like in a job, rated from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). I think these are the items themselves, although these (1975?) differ from the original in the 1974 tech report.
