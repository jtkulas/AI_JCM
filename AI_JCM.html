The Job Characteristics Model Revisited

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to alter the fundamental design and experience of work. The Job Characteristics Model (JCM; Hackman & Oldham, 1975, 1976) proposes that core job characteristics shape critical psychological states and, ultimately, important employee outcomes. Emerging research suggests that digitization may substantially alter these characteristics. For example, digital technologies may automate repetitive tasks, but they may also fragment work, limit skill use, or diminish employees’ sense of ownership over their work. Arora and Garg’s (2025) review of meaningful work in the digital age identifies autonomy, skill variety, feedback, task significance, and task identity as job characteristics affected by workplace digitization. As AI’s role in the design and performance of work grows it is important to understand how employees engage with the technology.
Conceptualizing and measuring employee AI use is challenging. Existing research operationalizes AI use in terms of the extent or intensity it is used to perform work. For example, Zhang et al. (2026) measure AI usage with items assessing the degree to which employees use AI in carrying out their job functions. Increasing AI integration may alter employees’ routines, role boundaries, autonomy, and skills, suggesting that the potential impact of AI use likely goes beyond frequency or intensity. Two employees could report similarly high levels of AI use while interacting with the technology in fundamentally different ways. One employee may primarily use AI to automate routine administrative work, another may use it to generate and refine ideas, and still another may engage with AI iteratively as a source of expertise or cognitive challenge. Accordingly, understanding AI-enabled work may require measures that capture how they incorporate AI into their work.Callari and Puppione (2025) provide one framework for distinguish forms of AI use. Drawing on qualitative data from employees using Microsoft 365 Copilot, they identified three ideal types of AI users Efficiency-Seeking, Pragmatic Integrators, and Collaborative Optimizers. These ideal types imply qualitatively different relationships between AI and features of work including autonomy, skill utilization, cognitive effort, and decision-making. The Callari and Puppione (2025) framework suggests that AI use is not a uniform behavior but rather is best considered multidimensional.Yet, translating qualitative ideal types into quantitative measures presents a challenge. Ideal types represent recurring patterns of behavior and experience, but they do not necessarily correspond to distinct psychological constructs. Additional work is needed to determine whether AI user types such as those presented by Callari and Puppione (2025) can be measured with survey items. Interestingly, the measurement of AI use is potentially enriched by recent technological developments. For a long time, the scale development process has relied on the efforts of researchers and subject-matter experts to turn construct definitions to survey items measuring the construct. Now, generative AI now provides tools for producing surveys that measure constructs from idea generation to item testing. However, the question of whether survey items produced by generative AI can properly measure constructs. The present study is an exploratory comparison of three approaches to developing items intended to assess employees’ orientations toward workplace AI use. Specifically, we compare items generated through (a) ChatGPT based on Callari and Puppione’s (2025) ideal AI user types, (b) AI Genie, and (c) industrial-organizational psychology subject-matter experts. We evaluate the internal consistency and underlying factor structure of each item set. In doing so, this study has two primary objectives: first, to provide evidence regarding whether qualitatively derived forms of human–AI interaction can be operationalized as quantitative constructs, and second, to examine whether different approaches to item generation yield comparable representations of those constructs. More broadly, establishing how employees differ in their use of AI represents an important step toward understanding how AI may reshape established features of job design and the psychological experience of work.

Research Question: Does one instrument development procedure emerge as better?

Procedure

I am interested in the three AI user ideal types and the survey items that could be generated to measure an employee’s propensity toward one type or another.”

SME

The traditional items were generated by paper authors. The three adopter types were treated as individual constructs, with definitions provided by Callari and Puppione (2025). An initial list of 57 candidate items was reduced to 16 through consensus evaluation of item clarity and content domain coverage.

Results

Exploratory factor analyses (EFAs) were conducted separately for each of the three item sets: ChatGPT–generated, subject-matter expert (SME)–generated, and AI Genie–generated. One, two, and three-factor solutions were estimated for each item set using maximum likelihood estimation and an oblique Quartimin rotation. Following Hu and Bentler (1999)’s guidelines, model fit was evaluated using multiple indicators including the chi-square test of model fit, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Across all three item sets, model fit improved significantly as additional factors were extracted. Parallel analysis, however, suggested different levels of dimensionality across the item sets, with a one-factor solution for the AI Genie and SME item sets and a two-factor solution for the ChatGPT item set. Because the item pools were constructed to represent three conceptual domains, the three-factor solutions are discussed here to evaluate the pattern of item loadings and correspondence between the intended and emergent factor structures. The three-factor solution for the AI Genie-generated items demonstrated substantially better fit compared to the one- and two-factor models, χ²(150) = 207.64, p = .001, RMSEA = .064, 90% CI [.041, .084], CFI = .960, TLI = .944, and SRMR = .036. Item loading patterns provided partial evidence of a clear three-factor structure. Factor 1 was defined by four EF items and two PI items: GENIE_EF_2 (.643), GENIE_EF_3 (.853), GENIE_EF_5 (.769), GENIE_EF_7 (.760), GENIE_PI_1 (.702), and GENIE_PI_4 (.744). The second factor included items from all groups of items with significant loadings occurring for GENIE_PI_2 (.804), GENIE_PI_3 (.868), GENIE_PI_6 (.670), GENIE_PI_7 (.658), GENIE_CO_3 (.728), GENIE_CO_4 (.625), and GENIE_CO_5 (.693). Additionally, items GENIE_EF_1, GENIE_EF_6, and GENIE_PI_5, had significant cross loadings on Factors 1 and 2. Compared to Factors 1 and 2, Factor 3 was weakly defined. GENIE_CO_6 demonstrated the clearest loading on this factor (.562), whereas GENIE_CO_1 had a smaller loading of (.388). GENIE-EF_4 which significantly loaded onto Factor 2 had a significant negative loading on Factor 3 (-.310). Factor 1 and Factor 2 had a significant correlation (r = .762, p < .05), whereas the correlations of Factor 3 with Factors 1 (r = .295) and 2 (r = .289) did not approach statistical significance. Overall, the three-factor model demonstrated relatively good fit, the loading pattern suggests two somewhat clear factors with item cross loadings and an ambiguous third factor.

The three-factor solution for the SME-generated items demonstrated the strongest overall model fit of the three item-generation approaches, χ²(150) = 183.17, p = .034, RMSEA = .048, 90% CI [.015, .071], CFI = .962, TLI = .947, and SRMR = .050. Factor 1 was defined by PI items, including SME_PI_1 (.521) and SME_PI_2 (.597), SME_PI_4 (.656), SME_PI_6 (.595), and SME_PI_7 (.564), as well as SME_CO_1 (.629) and SME_CO_5 (.407). Additionally, four items including SME_ES_3 (-.559), SME_ES_5 (-.574), and SME_ES_7 (-.630) loaded negatively onto this factor. Items SME_ES_1 (.740) and SME_PI_3 (.724) loaded mostly strongly on Factor 2, with SME_ES_6 exhibiting a more modest loading (.351). Factor 3 was primarily defined by SME_PI_5 (.790) and several CO items, including SME_CO_2 (.402), SME_CO_3 (.553), SME_CO_4 (.411), SME_CO_6 (.672), and SME_CO_7 (.584). SME_ES_4 exhibited loadings on both Factor 1 (-.320) and Factor 3 (.388), while SME_ES_2 did not load strongly on any single factor. All three factors were significantly correlated at the 5% level threshold (p < .05) . Factor 1 correlated .441 with Factor 2 and .635 with Factor 3, while Factors 2 and 3 correlated .458. Overall, the three-factor model provided strong fit, although the like the AI_Genie items, loadings did not create three distinct item groupings.
Like the other two item sets, model fit improved as additional factors were extracted for the ChatGPT-generated items. The three-factor model provided the best fit of the models examined for the ChatGPT-Generated items, χ²(207) = 347.09, p < .001, RMSEA = .084, 90% CI [.068, .099], CFI = .879, TLI = .838, and SRMR = .053. Despite this improvement, the additional indicators of model fit (RMSEA, CFI, and TLI) indicated relatively worse fit than the three-factor solutions for the SME and AI_Genie models.

Nonetheless, the three-factor solution revealed several interpretable item loadings. Factor 1 was primarily defined by ES items, including chat_ES_1 (.472), chat_ES_3 (.769), chat_ES_6 (.680), and chat_ES_8 (.453), and chat_PI_3 (.495) which also had a significant negative cross-loading with Factor 3 (-.303). Factor 2 was defined by the PI and CO items. PI item loadings on Factor 2 included chat_PI_1 (.751), chat_PI_4 (.765), chat_PI_6 (.600), chat_PI_7 (.601), and chat_PI_8 (.464). All eight CO items loaded most strongly on Factor 2, with loadings ranging from .490 to .864. Finally, Factor 3 was defined by a handful of ES items, including chat_ES_4 (.520), chat_ES_5 (.650), and chat_ES_8 (.490). Notably, chat_ES_8 cross-loaded on Factors 1 (.453) and 3 (.490), while chat_ES_7 did not load strongly on any factor. Factors 1 and 2 were significantly correlated (r = .477, p < .05), while Factor 3 was did not have a significant correlation with Factor 1 (r = -.029) or Factor 2 (r = -.146). Taken together, these results suggest that the ChatGPT-generated items exhibited meaningful multidimensionality, but the observed structure did not cleanly produce loadings indicating three distinct factors. Additionally, the ChatGPT-generated items demonstrated worse model fit than the corresponding SME and AI Genie item sets.
